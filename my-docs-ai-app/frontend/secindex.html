Exploring the Social Science Disciplines

Economics and Political Science in Higher Education Policy

Overview of the Discipline: Economics

  • Economics is the social science that studies how people, firms, and governments allocate limited resources and weigh the costs and benefits of decisions such as investing in higher education.

Two Key Theories in Economics

  • Human capital theory: Eide and Showalter (2010) explain that this theory treats education the same way economists treat a business investing in new equipment, since a person takes on the direct and indirect costs of schooling now in exchange for higher earnings and better opportunities later in life. Reber and Turner (2026) apply this same logic to student loans, showing how income driven repayment plans let a borrower spread the cost of their education across a career instead of paying it all up front.
  • Moral hazard and risk sharing theory: McGuire and van Kleef (2018) describe risk sharing as an arrangement where a regulator absorbs part of the financial risk in a system so that the people or organizations inside it are protected from uncertainty, though this protection can also weaken their incentive to control costs. Dandridge (2026) and Reber and Turner (2026) apply this same tension to higher education, since a university or a borrower who knows part of the cost will eventually be covered by the government may make different choices about tuition or borrowing than they would if they bore the full cost themselves.

Purpose and Importance of Economics, with Example

  • Economics matters here because it helps measure whether the money spent on college produces a real return for students and taxpayers, and it explains how repayment policy shapes who can actually afford to enroll. A clear example is the shift from the SAVE plan to the new Repayment Assistance Plan, which calculates monthly payments differently depending on whether a graduate earns a modest income or a high income after finishing school.

Overview of the Discipline: Political Science

  • Political science studies how governments, agencies, laws, and interest groups make decisions and distribute resources like access to higher education across society.

Two Key Theories in Political Science

  • Institutionalism, sometimes called the regulatory state approach: Hoefer (2022) states "structures endure while individuals come and go" (para. 9). This idea is central to institutionalism and explains why policy tends to stay in place even as administrations change. Dandridge (2026) applies this directly, comparing how the Office for Students in England and the Department of Education in the United States each try to define and enforce academic quality through binding rules rather than informal expectations.
  • Agenda setting theory, closely related to policy feedback theory: Mohn (2022) states "the media does not necessarily tell people what they should think" (para. 5). This same logic extends to how policymakers set the agenda for what gets debated and legislated. Reber and Turner (2026) trace this pattern through federal loan policy, showing how litigation, executive actions, and new legislation such as the One Big Beautiful Bill Act keep shifting what repayment options exist depending on which party controls Congress and the courts.

Purpose and Importance of Political Science, with Example

  • Political science matters because it explains how rules get written, how agencies carry them out, and how shifts in political power change who has access to things like affordable college. A strong example is how state attorneys general successfully challenged an executive loan forgiveness plan in court, which then forced Congress to pass new legislation overriding the earlier rules.

Key Takeaway

  • Dandridge (2026) states "quality is a fundamental principle of higher education" (para. 1). That single line ties both disciplines together, since economics asks whether that quality produces a measurable return and political science asks who gets to define and enforce it in the first place.
